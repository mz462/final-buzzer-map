    Final Buzzer: crowd-aware routing home after a championship at Madison Square Garden

    Final Buzzer is a mobile-first, real-time egress planner for fans leaving a major event in Midtown Manhattan. Pick where you're going and how you're traveling: walk, transit (subway, PATH, LIRR, NJ Transit) or drive. The app fuses official alerts, social media posts and on-site reports into confidence-scored street closures. It reroutes you around the closures it trusts and tells you when to leave, not just which way to go.

    Built for the Plug and Play × PMAI Hackathon, Challenge 2: "Knicks win the championship. Get fans home safely from MSG." All data in this prototype is mocked. The scenario is a home title clinch at MSG, which ends at 10:30 PM. "Now" is 11:15 PM.

    The problem

    • MSG holds 19,812 fans for basketball and sits directly on top of Penn Station, the busiest station in North America at roughly 600,000 riders a day.
    • When the Knicks won the 2026 title on June 13, they clinched on the road in San Antonio. Even so, tens of thousands of fans filled Midtown from 5th to 9th Ave. Press reports counted 63 arrests and 10 officers injured. An ambulance could not get through the crowd to reach a teenager who had been shot near 43rd St and Broadway.
    • In May 2026, a watch party at 33rd St and 7th Ave drew about 6,000 people (NYPD estimate). The crowd blocked 33rd St, 34th St and 7th Ave.
    • Similar nights elsewhere: the Toronto Raptors parade in 2019 drew about 2 million people and shut subway stations for overcrowding, stranding thousands of commuters.
    • A home title clinch at MSG would put all of that right on top of Penn Station. Nobody knows which streets are actually closed, and rumors spread faster than official alerts.

    Hero feature 1: the Escape Window ("delay to save time")

    For every departure time from now to +45 minutes, Final Buzzer adds up four parts:

    • the forecast queue to exit the arena (or the parking garage);
    • the walk or drive through the streets that are still open;
    • the forecast platform crowding at each station;
    • the ride itself.

    It then recommends the departure that gets you home earliest. Often the answer is "wait 15–25 minutes and leave later." Crowds clear faster than you can push through them, so waiting can mean arriving sooner while spending far less time in the crush. Research on crowd evacuation backs this up: a 2020 study in Royal Society Open Science found that staggering exits based on live conditions cut peak crowd density by about 35% and added only about 5% to total exit time. Every staggered departure also takes pressure off the crowd for everyone else. No consumer navigation app we found tells you to "leave later, arrive sooner."

    Hero feature 2: destination-aware station choice

    Choose a destination: Upper West Side, Hoboken, Brooklyn, Long Island or Murray Hill. For each destination, the app compares every reasonable station. For example:

    • Penn Station's 7th Ave entrance (1/2/3, LIRR) against its 8th Ave entrance (A/C/E);
    • Moynihan Train Hall, 34 St–Herald Sq (B/D/F/M/N/Q/R/W), Times Sq–42 St, 28 St, and PATH at 33rd St or 23rd St.

    Each station is scored on walk time around confirmed closures, forecast platform crowding and train time. If an entrance is exit-only, or the only way to reach a station is through a crowd surge, the app rules that station out and shows why. Drivers get tunnel choices instead (Lincoln vs Holland, Queens–Midtown). Each travel mode shows only the incidents that matter for it: a closed subway entrance doesn't clutter a driver's map, and car gridlock doesn't clutter a walker's.

    How confidence works: the JEV fusion model

    Every incident gathers signals from several sources. Each signal has a base weight:

    • official agency alerts (NYPD, MTA, Port Authority): 0.95, with no decay;
    • on-site reports from app users: 0.40;
    • posts with photos: 0.35;
    • text-only posts: 0.15.

    Non-official signals lose weight over time, with a 60-minute time constant. The signals are combined with a noisy-OR: conf = 1 − ∏(1 − wᵢ·e^(−ageᵢ/τ)).

    • An incident becomes "confirmed" and blocks routing at a confidence of 0.70 or more.
    • An official denial marks a rumor as debunked. In the demo, the "Lincoln Tunnel closed?" rumor is ignored at confidence 0.15 and then debunked by Port Authority.
    • An official reopen clears the incident.

    The Jev decision model is used as the per-post classifier. It reads each post or report, extracts the location and incident type, and judges how credible it is. The transparent fusion formula above produces the final score. You can tap any incident to see its full calculation, source by source. The model is explainable rather than a black box.

    Planned improvements:

    • Count duplicate reposts of the same post only once.
    • Weight each reporter by how accurate their past reports turned out to be.
    • Forecast when each crowd will clear, using camera-based crowd counts and outflow rates.

    Mock APIs in this demo, and the real feeds they stand in for

    • Street closures: NYC Open Data street closures (Socrata API) plus 511NY events. Official, machine-readable and free.
    • Traffic speeds: the NYC DOT Traffic Speeds feed, updated about every 5 minutes. This is the source for the 8th Ave gridlock signal.
    • Transit status: MTA GTFS-Realtime feeds for subway, LIRR and Metro-North, with service alerts (no API key). NJ Transit GTFS-RT. A community PATH realtime feed. These provide entrance closures such as "Penn 7th Ave exit-only" and platform delays.
    • Crowd signal: NYC DOT traffic camera stills around 31st–34th St, run through a vision crowd-count model.
    • Social: the Bluesky Jetstream firehose, which is free and real-time, filtered by keyword and location (MSG, Penn, 7th Ave, 34th St). X was left out of the MVP because of its streaming cost.
    • Official accounts: NYPD, Notify NYC and Port Authority posts, monitored as official signals.
    • Routing: in production, a self-hosted Valhalla router, with each confirmed closure passed in as an area to avoid. This prototype runs Dijkstra on a simplified Midtown grid with the same avoid rules.

    Why mock them? The demo has to be repeatable: the same scripted 45 minutes after the buzzer, replayable with the time slider. Each mock is shaped like the real feed's records (source, timestamp, location, text), so swapping in a live feed is an adapter, not a redesign.

    Try the demo

    1. Pick Brooklyn → Transit. Penn's 7th Ave entrance is exit-only and Herald Sq is cut off by the 34th St crowd surge. Leaving now, the app walks you south to the quiet 28 St station. The Escape Window says wait about 20 minutes: by then the A/C/E platforms at Penn's 8th Ave entrance have cleared, and you get home sooner. Tap the later bars to watch the station choice change and Herald Sq reopen in the forecast.
    2. Pick Hoboken → Drive. You'll see the Lincoln Tunnel rumor ignored, then debunked.
    3. Press Replay from buzzer to watch confidence build as posts, photos and on-site reports arrive.

    Who it's for

    • Fans leaving MSG on foot, by transit or by car.
    • Families who need a calm, low-crowd route.
    • Venue and city operations teams, who benefit when departures are spread out and emergency lanes stay clear.

    The same engine works for any large event at any venue: concerts, World Cup matches at MetLife, New Year's Eve, parades.
